# Source Verifier

Treat source quality as a property of a source **for a specific claim**, not as a permanent score for a domain.

## Research workflow

1. Split the request into the material claims that need external support. Mark claims where errors would change the user's decision.
2. Search for the strongest available evidence for each claim. Prefer, in order:
   - original records, official documentation, laws, standards, datasets, filings, and research papers;
   - authoritative institutions or named experts with direct responsibility for the subject;
   - reputable reporting that identifies its evidence and corrects errors;
   - secondary summaries only for context or discovery.
3. Open and inspect the source. Do not cite a search snippet, AI summary, copied press release, or aggregator as though it were the underlying evidence.
4. Evaluate whether the source actually supports the precise claim. Check authorship, publication date, methods or evidence, scope, incentives, and whether a newer version supersedes it.
5. For consequential, disputed, surprising, or rapidly changing claims, seek independent corroboration. Two pages repeating the same original report count as one source.
6. Resolve disagreements by comparing proximity to the event or data, relevant expertise, methods, date, and disclosed conflicts. Describe a material unresolved disagreement instead of hiding it.
7. Write claims no more strongly than the evidence permits. Place citations beside the supported claim and distinguish sourced facts, calculations, and inferences.

## Source selection rules

- Match the source to the claim. A vendor is authoritative about its current product documentation, but not necessarily about comparisons with competitors.
- Prefer primary sources, but do not assume they are neutral. Pair self-interested primary claims with independent evidence when the claim concerns effectiveness, impact, or superiority.
- Use recent sources when facts can change. Use older sources when they are the canonical record or the question is historical.
- Treat popularity, polished design, HTTPS, a familiar domain, and high search rank as weak signals. They do not establish correctness.
- Avoid anonymous, unverifiable, citation-free, scraped, or content-farm pages when stronger evidence exists.
- Never invent a citation or imply that a source was checked when only its title or snippet was seen.

For high-stakes research or close calls, read [references/evaluation-rubric.md](references/evaluation-rubric.md). Apply the rubric with judgment; do not turn its dimensions into a mechanical numeric score.

## Response requirements

Give the answer first. Cite every material externally verifiable claim close to where it appears. Briefly disclose important evidence gaps, source conflicts, or necessary reliance on a weaker source. If reliable evidence cannot be found, say what was searched and what remains unverified.
